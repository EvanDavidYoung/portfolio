I studied Spanish for three years in high school and figured learning Mandarin would be a harder version of the same thing. More vocabulary, stranger grammar, but same basic process: drill words, build sentences, talk to people.

Nobody mentioned that every syllable has four possible meanings depending on the pitch you use to say it.

## What tones actually are

Mandarin has four tones plus a neutral tone. The classic example everyone trots out is _mā_ (mother), _má_ (hemp), _mǎ_ (horse), _mà_ (scold). Same sound, four different words. Say the wrong one and you've told your host family you want to scold the soup.

The tones aren't accents. They're not emphasis. They're phonemic — as meaningful as the difference between _b_ and _p_ in English. When I heard them in isolation I could tell them apart fine. When I heard them in real speech, running together at normal speed, I had no idea what was happening.

## The first two months

My first two months in Taipei I was getting by on context, gestures, and the fact that Taiwanese people are relentlessly patient with foreigners butchering their language. I'd approximate the sounds and watch people's faces to see if they understood. Usually they did. I started to think tones weren't that important.

Then I tried to order at a breakfast shop without a menu to point at.

I asked for a 蛋餅 (dàn bǐng, egg crepe). I said something closer to 但病 (dàn bìng, but illness). The woman at the counter stared at me. I tried again. She stared harder. I pulled out my phone and typed it. She laughed, made the food, and said the word back to me very slowly.

I'd been saying the second syllable with a falling tone instead of a dipping one. For two months.

## What actually helped

**Shadowing.** I'd find a clip of someone speaking Mandarin — a podcast, a show, whatever — and try to mirror the sounds in real time without worrying about whether I understood the meaning. This forced me to pay attention to pitch contour rather than just consonants and vowels.

**Recording myself.** I hated this. I still hate this. But hearing a recording of how I actually sounded versus how I thought I sounded was useful enough that I kept doing it.

**A good tutor who would actually correct me.** This sounds obvious but the default in language exchange is for native speakers to just understand your intent and move on. I found a tutor who would stop me mid-sentence if I got a tone wrong and make me repeat it. Slower progress in conversation, faster progress in pronunciation.

**Anki with audio.** I stopped using text-only flashcards and made sure every card had audio from a native speaker. At some point my brain started tagging the sound of a word rather than the pinyin transcription.

## Where I am now

About eight months in, tones are mostly automatic for vocabulary I've learned properly. When I encounter a new word I hear the tones clearly and they stick. The problem is all the vocabulary I half-learned in the first few months — those words have shaky tonal foundations and I have to consciously rebuild them.

The other remaining issue is tone sandhi, particularly with 不 (bù) and 一 (yī), which change tone based on what follows them. I know the rules. I still sometimes have to think about it instead of just knowing. That gap between knowing a rule and having it feel natural is the current edge of my competence.

I'll probably be working on this for a long time. That's fine.
