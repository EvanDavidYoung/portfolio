---
title: 'One Year in Taiwan: What I Expected vs. What Happened'
pubDate: '2025-08-20'
---

I left a job I liked to move to a country where I didn't speak the language and knew basically nobody. Before I left, I made a list of things I expected to happen. I recently found that list and figured I'd go through it.

## What I expected

**I expected to be lonely.** I was, for about the first six weeks. Taiwan is one of the friendliest places I've ever been, but there's a difference between people being kind to you and feeling like you have a life somewhere. I spent a lot of evenings eating at the same three places near my apartment because I was too tired from studying to try anything new.

**I expected Taipei to feel smaller than New York.** It doesn't. The city is massive, dense in a different way than New York — you can be in a quiet residential lane and turn a corner into a ten-story night market. It took me months to feel like I had a mental map.

**I expected learning Chinese to be the main thing.** It was, structurally — I was studying three or four hours a day minimum. But it became less a thing I was doing and more a lens through which I was doing everything else. Getting lost and having to ask for directions was a Chinese lesson. Reading the menu was a Chinese lesson. Watching TV to wind down at night was a Chinese lesson. That was different from what I imagined.

**I expected to miss my career.** Somewhat — I missed the particular feeling of shipping something and watching people use it. I didn't miss the ambient anxiety of always being reachable, always being slightly behind on something.

## What I didn't expect

**I didn't expect to love the food this much.** I knew Taiwanese food was good. I did not anticipate that 滷肉飯 (braised pork rice) served at 7am would become one of my great pleasures in life, or that I'd start evaluating neighborhoods by the quality of their breakfast shops.

**I didn't expect the pace.** Things in Taipei move fast when they need to and slowly when they don't. Nobody is in a hurry to leave a restaurant. Nobody is annoyed that you're blocking the sidewalk while you figure out where you're going. This sounds small but it changes your whole nervous system after a while.

**I didn't expect to meet so many people in the same situation.** There's a whole ecosystem of people in Taipei who came for various reasons and found reasons to stay — language students, remote workers, people who were just passing through and then weren't. I found community faster than I thought I would, partly because everyone is figuring it out simultaneously.

**I didn't expect to have to decide whether to leave.** When I came I had a loose plan: one year, maybe extend to eighteen months, then figure out what's next. Around month ten I realized that leaving was going to feel like a choice I was making, not a natural end to a chapter. That's a harder feeling to sit with than I anticipated.

## The question people ask

Everyone asks whether it was worth it. I find this question hard to answer honestly because "worth it" implies a calculation that doesn't feel right.

I speak Mandarin now — not fluently, but well enough to have real conversations about things that matter. I know a city and how to move through it. I have a different relationship with the idea of starting over. I learned that I can be bad at something in public for a long time and survive it.

None of that fits neatly into "worth it." It just happened, and it changed what I'm like, and I'm glad.
